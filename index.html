Project Description

Our ultimate goal for Deceptacon is to build a hybrid robot that can use both legs and a rolling “wheel” mode to follow a target moving at two different speeds: fast and slow. Deceptacon has two core behaviors: it will walk to track a slow-moving target, such as a person or object, and it will switch into rolling mode when the target begins moving faster.

In order to do this, Deceptacon has a round, ball-like body with four trap doors that form a smooth exterior for rolling. When walking is more efficient, the trap doors open to reveal four legs. Each leg is powered by two servos: one controls the yaw axis, or side-to-side rotation, and another controls the pitch axis, or forward-and-back motion.

Along with these eight servos, we use two additional servos to animate large “ears” at the top of the robot. These ears act as stabilizers while the robot rolls, helping it move in a straight line. In total, we are using 10 servos: eight for the legs and two for the ears.

For sensors, we are using eight VL53L0X LIDAR sensors around the perimeter of the robot so it can detect its environment in both walking and rolling modes. This helps Deceptacon maintain steady motion and avoid obstacles while following its target.

In terms of power, we are connecting a 3300mAh LiPo battery in the center of the robot’s body, slightly elevated. Because of the battery’s weight, this placement helps with the rolling function. Finally, we plan on using an Arduino Nano to control the robot, though this is subject to change if a Raspberry Pi Zero proves to be more useful.

How We Came Up With Deceptacon

Initially, our goal was to design a bio-inspired armadillo robot, but we struggled to define a clear goal or task for it. After some brainstorming, we noticed similarities between an armadillo’s ability to curl and roll and the design of a Droideka from Star Wars, a robot that can roll to move quickly and unfold into a two-legged destroyer.

This led us to the idea of building a robot that can both roll and walk based on environmental conditions. Not only does this capture the armadillo’s unique movement, but it also connects to our in-class debate about the advantages of wheels versus legs.

Shown above is Team Deceptacon posing with the final version of our robot. Through plenty of trial and error, we successfully got all of the servos running and several of the sensors fully operational. One of our biggest takeaways from this project was learning that ambitious ideas are exciting, but much harder to achieve within the span of a single semester.

Overall, it was a rewarding experience collaborating as a team and bringing our vision to life. If we were to start the project again, we would focus on reducing the robot’s overall weight and reinforcing the legs earlier in the design process, since our greatest challenge was ensuring that the 3D-printed legs could support the robot’s weight.

Despite the obstacles, Deceptacon ultimately came to life, and we are proud of what we accomplished together. Cheers!
